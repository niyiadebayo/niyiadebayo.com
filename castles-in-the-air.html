Castles in the Air

An operator with three cars does not really need to understand his margin, and at that size it costs him almost nothing not to. He absorbs the leaks inside his own working day, drives himself when a driver fails to show, and the business survives arithmetic he has never done. The same man with fifty cars is losing money he cannot see, because a fleet carries its value in tiny bits daily, hundreds of small cash settlements that all have to be funneled somewhere they do not leak. At three vehicles he is the funnel. At fifty the leaks compound faster than he can walk the lot, and what the business needs from him instead is throughput, management capacity at every turn, and some sense of how to expand both without losing the tight ship.

So capital arrives looking for the operator who already runs at scale, with systems to inspect and financials worth underwriting, and in most of these markets that operator has never existed. Globally the successful fleet and rental businesses come down to a handful of companies, which tells you how hard the thing is to run even where the capital and the operating history are both sitting there. Here there is no audited record and no owner who can state his own unit economics with much confidence, so the job becomes choosing winners before the market has produced any.

The vector

When we showed up in Bogotá our kind of business was not known there, and what existed instead was something older and more disjointed. A driver took his licence from one owner and his car from somewhere else, and then worked under a rotation that decided which days of the week he was allowed on the road at all.

The licence is called a cupo. Bogotá caps them at 50,007, and they trade between owners at prices nobody publishes. Both the transport ministry and the city's mobility office hold that the word has no standing in Colombian regulation, so no rule creates a cupo and none governs what it costs.1 An asset worth real money, capped in supply, with no legal existence, which is the shape the informal stack describes. On top of that, pico y placa takes two plate digits off the road each day, Monday to Saturday, half past five in the morning until nine at night.2 Every taxi in the city loses a working day a week to a calendar.

The temptation on arrival is to change the kind of business being done, but that was never the search. We were looking for whoever was already at the far edge of stretching the system as it stood, and for the up-and-coming entrepreneurs at that same edge. Some were reworking the payout to licence owners, some pushing on driver financing, some aggregating cupos or aggregating cars to pool resources for scale. Nobody writes any of this down as strategy. It is what an operator improvises when the arrangement he inherited stops fitting what he wants to build.

The hunch is that your capacity to nudge someone is highest when he is already pointed in the vector direction of where you see the opportunity, and where your technology can do the most to expand what he is already doing. So you find whoever is moving the way your technology is about to make cheap, and you put weight behind the motion, capital and operating support alongside it, all of it worked backwards from the future you are underwriting.

Never one bet

We found our first partner in one of the oldest taxi companies in the country, which had new blood in management pointed at building something large. They had already written their own app, already started down a direct rent-to-own path, and already held a substantial share of the city's licences. The vector showed in work they had done before we ever walked in, which is the only evidence available when there is no other kind. That was a lucky streak and worth saying plainly. They were ready to co-create while keeping their identity distinct and sharpening their own operations.

But a single partner is a single point of failure for a market and not just for a deal. So alongside them we backed young entrepreneurs working bikes, pushing on driver financing, on collections, and on a daily experience good enough that drivers chose them over anyone else. We seeded the competition on purpose.

Most markets will not hand you an incumbent with new blood, and for those I use a formula of four hustlers, two outsiders, and two adjacent business people. The hustlers give you founder mode and learn quickest. The outsiders have their own reason to win and bring contrarian ideas to building operations from the ground up, the kind nobody inside the industry would try. The adjacent ones bring the acumen of proper business structure.

The point of eight is not really insurance. Different origin stories produce different approaches, and the operators watch each other try them, so the market ends up experimented on eight ways at once. That pushes the rate of experimentation up while keeping the odds of any one failed experiment growing proportionally large very low.

The trust layer

The most disciplined fleet operation I have seen came out of the outsider slot, which is the slot efficiency cuts first. An all-female-led operator, with instincts about management slightly more unorthodox than any typical hustler's.

She started from what actually mattered to the drivers on her fleet. Most of them were in their forties, and beyond the job they cared about their families and their kids, so her entire employee management was optimized for helping them reach better life outcomes, financially and relationally both. It began with helping them save a portion of their income, moved to helping them schedule shifts so they could be home when it mattered, and then she used the float from those same savings to build a credit system the drivers could tap into.

She built that float herself, out of the drivers' own money, in a market where no float is expected at all. The trust layer came together so fast that everyone wanted to drive for her fleet, and only once she was oversubscribed did the quality control arrive, the expectations and the standards that every other operator tries to impose in week one. She never had to impose discipline, because she made her fleet the one drivers wanted and let the excess demand do the enforcing, and the reputation that built is the kind no bank can copy and no competitor can buy.

The two ceilings

Of the eight, seven worked.3 The one that did not was a hustler who failed in transport and then made it later in a completely unrelated field, which for portfolio building is not a bad outcome at all. Selection turns out to be close to a solved problem. Of the seven, one got past the ceiling.

She stopped at three hundred cars, not beaten but comfortable. She had all she needed, and for an emerging market it was an outcome she was terribly satisfied with. Scale in these markets gets limited at a level of comfort long before it gets limited at a level of discipline. Comfort here buys a security worth disproportionately more when the floor beneath you sits lower, which is the same ladder in a soft-currency economy that makes every rung count for more than its equivalent elsewhere.

The other ceiling is competence, and it breaks in one specific place. Management span stops being able to sustain daily operations, and the team cannot train the next crop of managers, which is an organizational failure to reproduce rather than any limit on the owner's own ability.

The one who broke through had appetite from the start and was willing to do the work of stretching. For him stretching meant standing up operations in a new country the moment he felt confident enough in the first, and then doing it again. He exercised that muscle faster and faster, so the feedback loops got shorter and he learned quicker for it. More and more people went through his school of management, until the school itself was codified into a repeatable playbook. He never widened his own span so much as removed span as the constraint.

Her method was every bit as codifiable. Savings can be opted into, details on the kids collected, school fees automated out of the float. What she ran out of was appetite, and appetite is the one thing capital can neither build nor teach, which makes it the deciding variable in all of this.

Castles in the air

Which turns the question of which operator is allowed to grow into a question about reading appetite in a room, before anything has happened to prove it.

He had it from the beginning because he already had a story for global expansion on day one, even though he did not yet know how to build it. I call that building castles in the air, and then rolling your sleeves to do the work as if you never thought of a castle in the air. Both halves have to be there. The story on its own is a dreamer whose castle never touches an operation, and the work on its own is a very good operator carrying a story sized to the life he already has, which is the shape of every ceiling in the portfolio.

With no track record, talk is all you have, so you read different things from talk pointed in different directions. The forward story you check for size, and the question is whether it is larger than anything this person can currently build. The backward story you check for grain, because the sleeves are in the stories of what he has built before. The more detail he gets into about a business he has already run, the numbers, the specific mess, the month something went wrong, the more credible he is as a builder and not just air puffing. A fake clears the first test easily. Nobody clears the second without having been there.

Within proximity

The other thing to read at a first meeting is how he is building his current team. If you cannot look at each of them and think this one could just as well be a founder, the team will not go far. Problems in these markets arrive from inside and outside at once, so you need people who think on their feet and make decisions fast, like the business belongs to them, with alignment that is implicit and a great deal of accountability behind it. Command and control cannot keep pace with both directions.

You live with the risk that they walk. The hallmark of a great operator is that he can convince these otherwise founder-type people to build a large story with him instead of building their own individual small ones. One operation I watched break in Accra broke exactly there. Management fell out with the founder somewhere along the line, and the founder did not have the grace to seed those managers into their new venture, which is what could have kept the compounding within proximity. Instead it turned into a rat race to the bottom.

Seeding is investing in them, or handing them equity in operations, or both, to build an adjacency that is large. The split itself is survivable, and what does the damage is refusing to finance it. Compounding concentrates in a place, and it does the same in a network of people, so you keep them within proximity and it stays close enough to count.

Which leaves the honest accounting, and it is worth doing plainly. Eight bets, seven working businesses, one operator past the ceiling. What the intervention buys you is a higher ceiling rather than a scaled operator, and that distinction matters. Each of those seven ended up running something several times larger than anything they had ever actually run before, then stopped, satisfied, at a number that would have looked impossible to them at the start. Capacity rises one comfort point at a time. Slower than the story capital tells itself, and the only version that has actually happened.
