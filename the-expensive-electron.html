The Expensive Electron

In 2022 Angola spent about $3.8 billion holding down the price of fuel. That was 92 percent of what the country spent that year on education and health together. Sonangol was buying petrol at around 583 kwanza a litre and selling it at 160.1 In 2023 the government started unwinding it and the pump price nearly doubled, from 160 kwanza to 300. Diesel went up again in 2025.2

Now price it in the money a driver's costs are actually set in. At the start of 2020, 160 kwanza was about 33 US cents. Today 300 kwanza, at 915 to the dollar, is about 33 US cents.3 The pump price doubled. The currency halved. Nothing moved.

Angola did the hard thing and the signal never arrived.

Someone had already bet the other way. T'Leva, a Luanda ride-hailing company, announced more than $20 million in 2020 to put Chinese electric cars on the road with Ledo Holding. By August 2021 it was running more than 400 of them, employing over 700 people directly, and aiming at 10,000 active electric vehicles on the platform by 2025, with Benguela, Lubango and Huambo next and every SADC country after that.4

It never got there. The Android listing is gone. The iPhone app has 35 ratings on Angola's App Store and last shipped a build in 2023.5 The company still trades. It just stopped being the thing it said it would be, about 96 percent short of the number it named. My own company operates in Luanda, so read that as a competitor's story told by a competitor.

The cars were not what failed. In 2021 a driver in Luanda was buying petrol at 160 kwanza that the state was purchasing at 583. No electric vehicle wins that argument, and no amount of operating skill closes a gap the treasury is holding open.

I keep going back to that because of what is happening upstream. Chinese manufacturers exported a record 540,000 electric vehicles in July, the largest month BYD has ever had, while sales inside China fell 12 percent over the first seven months of the year.6 The cars are cheap, they are good, and they are looking for somewhere to go. The story writes itself as a leapfrog. Poor countries skip the combustion era the way they skipped landlines.

I have argued before why that framing fails for physical AI, and it fails here for the same reason. It reads a cost story as a technology story. Manufacturing is solved. Logistics is mostly solved. Both are commodities now, and that is the problem. The solved problems only make the unsolved problems much more evident.

The entry condition

The question to ask about a market is not income, or urbanisation, or even whether the grid works. What does a litre cost against what a kilowatt-hour costs, in the money the driver earns?

Angola shows why the obvious version of that test is wrong. Petrol there is still among the cheapest anywhere, and electricity is cheaper still, two and a half to three US cents a kilowatt-hour, third lowest in Africa.7 The electric case works there now, not because petrol got expensive, but because the state subsidises the electron harder than it subsidises the litre.

Tajikistan is the same test at the other end. It has the most expensive petrol in Central Asia and close to the cheapest electricity in the world. In 2022 it had 120 electric vehicles out of about 445,000 cars, three hundredths of one percent. That October it exempted them from all duties and taxes for ten years. By 2025 it had 34,354, about 5 percent of everything on the road.8

So the condition is a ratio, and it is necessary. It is not sufficient. Nigeria removed its petrol subsidy in 2023 and passes the test on paper. Nigeria is where the argument breaks.

What a kilowatt costs

Take the car actually being bought at this end of the market. A Wuling Bingo is rated at 9.58 kWh per 100 kilometres.9 Call it 12 in stop-start traffic with the air conditioning running.

On Nigeria's Band A tariff at 209.50 naira a unit, that is about 2,500 naira to move the car 100 kilometres. Eight litres of petrol at 1,200 is 9,600. Nearly four times cheaper, and on that arithmetic nobody would hesitate.

Band A means twenty hours of supply a day. Most urban households see about four.10 So the electron a driver can reach is one he makes himself. A petrol generator burns half a litre to seven tenths of a litre for each kilowatt-hour,11 which puts his electricity between 600 and 840 naira a unit against the 209 on the tariff sheet. The same 100 kilometres now costs him 7,200 to 10,080 naira. The fourfold advantage is gone. He did not buy a cheaper car. He bought a car whose fuel he has to manufacture at three times the retail price. Public fast charging in Lagos, at about 35 US cents a unit,12 leaves him a fifth better off than petrol, which is not a reason to change anything.

Ethiopia is the counter-case, and it is instructive about scale. More than 140,000 electric vehicles have been imported. The utility's first phase of public charging is 40 stations, 32 of them in Addis, against a national target of 2,230. The ones that opened this year are at Kotebe, Yelebu and Piassa. Electricity still reaches about half the population.13 The state also wrote a separate charging tariff, 14 to 18 birr a unit, against a residential rate that starts under one birr.14 That is a government deciding on purpose what the transition should cost.

What this means for one person: Ampersand, which runs battery swapping for motorcycle taxis from a depot on KK 6 Av in Kigali, says a petrol rider spends more than $11 a day on fuel and vehicle leasing and takes home as little as $1.60.15 Those are the company's own figures and should be read as such. But that is the shape of it. The electron takes most of what a man earns. A tariff sheet will never show you that.

Nobody to fix it

An electric car has fewer moving parts, so it should break less. It does. The trouble is what happens when it breaks anyway.

Every mechanic in these cities was trained on combustion. The knowledge is deep, it is informal, and none of it transfers. A man who can rebuild a carburettor in an afternoon cannot open a battery pack or read a controller fault. Without a dealership or a flagship workshop in the country, diagnosis becomes guesswork, and the car sits while people guess.

The repair bill is the smaller half of what that costs. The bigger half is the revenue the driver never earns while the car sits, and in most rent-to-own structures the repair lands on him as well, while the payment schedule carries on.16 In Kenya, riders lose up to 500 shillings, about four dollars, on a single missed swap.17 Multiply that by the days a car waits for a part.

There is a second-order version almost nobody prices. These markets run on used vehicles. An electric car whose battery is a third of its value, degrading on a curve nobody locally can certify, has no resale market yet. No residual value means no financing structure. The maintenance gap does not only idle cars. It caps what the asset class can be lent against at all.

Only the asset

In most of these markets there is no real equity, because the legibility work has never been done. I have written elsewhere about how a platform makes a transport company's vehicles visible enough for a bank to underwrite them. The same logic runs down to the driver. What is real is the productive asset, and the asset stands in for equity. Hence the wave of rent-to-own.

The capital has arrived on that basis. Moove raised $250 million in August at a $2.1 billion valuation, led by Mubadala, financing cars for drivers working on ride-hailing platforms. Watu, one of the largest non-bank asset financiers on the continent, posted net profit of 4.8 billion shillings for 2025 on revenue up 92.7 percent to 28.3 billion.18 Both are real businesses built on the same insight, that the asset is the only collateral there is.

What those numbers do not tell you is what happened to the driver. A financier's margin and a borrower's balance are separate questions, and the second one is much harder to find published anywhere. That gap is why the objection further down has teeth.

How badly the legibility is missing shows up somewhere odd. Try to establish what share of the cars in a city are working vehicles rather than private ones. The registries cannot tell you. The Philippine split between private and for-hire is not cleanly published. Colombia's public-service share covers cars and leaves out the motorcycles that are most of its fleet. Nigeria's own tables disagree with each other, and at least one of them counts a different population altogether.19 Three countries, three incompatible categories, three different years. You cannot compare them, and that you cannot compare them is the finding. This is what an illegible market looks like from the inside, and it is why capital prices these assets the way it does.

Rent the battery

If you have to build the missing layer anyway, build it as something you own.

Start with what costs money. Battery packs are down to about $84 a kilowatt-hour in China and roughly $105 as a global average.20 On a car selling for nine to twelve thousand dollars, a 40 kWh pack is between 28 and 37 percent of the vehicle. So stop selling the battery with the car. Sell the car, rent the battery, charge for the swap. Third-party estimates put the saving at $500 to $1,000 off the purchase.21

That does two things, and the second matters more. It takes a third off the down payment, which puts the vehicle in reach of many more people much sooner. And it moves the most expensive failure in the vehicle off the driver's balance sheet and onto the network's.

The objection to all of this is Acemoglu's. Technology arriving does not distribute its own gains. They go to whoever holds power over the direction, and historically they were shared only when people organised and forced the sharing.22 What I am describing has an operator owning the swap network, setting the specification, and taking a margin on every swap. I am not going to pretend it is something else.

What makes it defensible is the split. The driver owns the car. He rents the consumable. Nobody thinks buying petrol makes a man a tenant. It is a fair trade because it is productivity acceleration, money cycling faster into people's pockets, which ends up pushing consumption. It stops being fair the moment the instrument hands the risk back to him, which is exactly what the repair clause does today.

The mechanics are further along than most people think. CATL passed its two thousandth standardised swap station at the end of June and is adding more than 200 a month, on packs built to work across brands and vehicle classes. The first production car on that standard went to a taxi fleet, a thousand Changan Oshan 520s.23 Spiro runs 2,500 swap stations across six African countries.24 Read Spiro carefully, though. A motorcycle pack is a few kilowatt-hours and a man carries it in one hand. A 40 kWh car pack is a different physical and capital problem, and sliding between the two is how people talk themselves into projects that do not work. Better Place raised around $850 million on swapping and died of that exact gap.

Two things decide whether it happens. The first is permission. In Nigeria, generating under a megawatt for your own use is a registration, but selling a kilowatt-hour to anyone else needs a licence whatever the size.25 In Côte d'Ivoire it sits inside a concession granted in 1990 and extended in 2020.26 A swap network is a company selling electricity to third parties. That is the barrier, not the batteries.

The second is density. You need fleet density first and interoperability after it, and whoever reaches density first is who leads the interoperability. What emerges looks like an app store. Open enough that anyone can build on it, owned enough that the owner takes a cut and decides who gets in.

The art and the science

States have to iterate faster on what is already working. Ethiopia works. Tajikistan works, and its five-year programme funds maintenance capacity and battery recycling rather than only the tariff, which is the whole ecosystem written into policy by the one government that got it right.8

The rest is generation. Couple this to renewables, let people produce and sell power at scale, and simplify getting the surplus onto the grid. South Africa stopped raising its generation licensing threshold and removed it altogether.27 Kenya gazetted net metering and then found the utility could not credit exported power, so it went into a pilot.28 One of those is a state getting out of the way. The other wrote the rule and never built the road from the meter to the money.

Six years after T'Leva ordered its first Chinese cars, the mobility money in Luanda has gone somewhere else. Anda raised $3.4 million in November to put motorcycle taxis on a drive-to-own footing, and it does not own a fleet. It finances riders.29 The asset sits with the person using it.

Building here is as much art as science. The art is reading the policy winds and adapting quickly. The science is getting rigorous about what makes the math work in each specific market, then going after it aggressively. Neither half survives without the other, and the puzzle only fits as a whole.

I am watching Addis do this properly. I would like to see it done in twenty more cities.
